# What your estimates have in common

Back of the Envelope · Week 8

---

## Errors that differ partly cancel

Ask ten people and the errors point in different directions — for reasons
particular to each of them. Put the estimates together and those
differences work against each other.

That is the whole mechanism. It only works when the errors actually differ.

## Five polls, unusually close, all wrong the same way

May 2019: every published poll had Labor winning the Australian federal
election. The Coalition won.

An inquiry found this met the criteria of a **polling failure**, not a
miss — the polls erred in the same direction, at a similar level, and the
error was in the polls themselves.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> "…no direct evidence of herding…"

The inquiry into the 2019 polls, on five separate organisations with five
separate methods.

```notes
Full framing: "While the Panel found no direct evidence of herding, this
cannot be ruled out... In the absence of any direct evidence of herding, the
most likely explanation is a systematic skew in the polls coupled with a
confirmation bias." The shared difficulty: "even though the methods used by
the pollsters differ they share a common difficulty in struggling to
establish contact with and gain the cooperation of a representative sample
of voters."
```

## More data does not dilute a shared mistake

It reproduces it — a shared input passes through every estimate at full
strength, no matter how many you collect.

The check is procedural, not statistical: did they start from the same
figure, the same dataset, the same training, the same room?

---

## And the room you are in

Your own estimates this semester were made after the same lectures, the
same worked examples, the same reference points.

Where they agree, some of that is real — and some of it may be the course.
Worth examining, not something anyone can settle for you.
