## Four questions, one number

- **Precision** — how finely it is written
- **Accuracy** — how close it sits to the thing
- **Uncertainty** — the spread it is really known within
- **Sufficiency** — whether it is good enough for *this* decision

Only the last one is yours to settle. Settle it first.

---

## What the count missed

- Census count — 25,417,978
- PES estimate of who should have been counted — 25,608,022
- Net undercount — **190,044 persons, or 0.7%**

The lowest net undercount recorded for an Australian census. It was 1.0% in
2016.

Better than you guessed, probably.

# 17.4%

2021 Census net undercount for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

```notes
The same census. The same methodology.

The PES estimated 983,257 people should have been counted against a Census
count of 812,505 --- a shortfall of 170,752 persons. The 2016 figure was 17.5%.

Take this slowly, and don't play it as a reveal. The point is not that
statistics are unreliable; it is that a count's coverage differs by population,
and every process that uses the count inherits that difference.
```

## Which population is the decision about?

Two teaching exercises --- not descriptions of any real allocation process:

**A.** A national planning task sized in millions: do a few tenths of a percent
change the decision?

**B.** A service for a community of a few thousand, counted with substantially
higher uncertainty: does the same relative error change it?

```notes
The digits look identical either way --- that is the whole point. Push them to
say what precision each decision would actually require.
```
