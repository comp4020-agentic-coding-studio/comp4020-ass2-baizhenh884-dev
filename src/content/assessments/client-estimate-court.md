Nothing about this target is in the future. You are not forecasting anything —
you are measuring something that already happened and was never counted
directly.

## The brief

> How many residential dwellings in the City of Melbourne were unoccupied for
> more than six months during calendar year 2026?

Every student answers the same question, for the same city, for the same
year. 2026 has already finished by the time you read this. The number exists.
Nobody publishes it.

## Why this target

Victoria's Vacant Residential Land Tax applies to residential land vacant for
more than six months in the preceding calendar year, and that half-year does
not need to be continuous — the test is whether the property was actually
lived in, not whether it was merely listed for sale or rent. Before the tax's
2025 expansion statewide, it already applied to a defined set of inner and
middle Melbourne councils that included the City of Melbourne itself, so this
LGA has sat inside the tax's scope for years before the one you are
estimating.

You are not estimating tax liability, and this brief is not a tax exercise.
You are estimating a count. **You must not identify, imply, or classify any
individual property or owner** — that is a matter for the Commissioner of
State Revenue, not for this assessment. Exemptions and individual enforcement
decisions are entirely outside scope. What you are producing is a city-wide
aggregate, built the way an analyst without direct access to SRO records
would have to build one.

## What you have, and what it is not

The nearest published figure is the Census. On Census night 2021 the City of
Melbourne recorded 26,072 unoccupied private dwellings out of 103,029. The
ABS's own dictionary is exact about what that means:

> "An 'Unoccupied' private dwelling is most often a separate house, townhouse,
> apartment or flat, with no one staying there on Census Night... a form with
> one or more people present on Census Night is set to 'occupied'; a form
> with all usual residents away on Census Night is set to 'unoccupied'."

That is one night, once every five years. The target you are estimating is a
different kind of quantity entirely: not who happened to be away on one
date, but who was absent, cumulatively, for more than half of an entire year.
A dwelling can be empty on Census night and lived in every other week. A
dwelling can hold a Census-night guest and stand empty the rest of the year.
Neither the Census figure nor the six-month test measures the other, and nor
does 2026 have a Census at all — 2021 is the nearest one you have, five years
removed from your target year. This is Week 5's bridge, built for real,
against a real gap.

If you reach for older ABS data that once recorded the reason a dwelling was
unoccupied — holiday home, for sale, temporarily absent — say so plainly and
treat it as what it is: a much older collection method, not current evidence.
Using it is optional, and it does not need to appear in every chain.

## What Weeks 5 to 9 give you

- **Week 5** — the proxy-to-target bridge, and the discipline of naming its
  breaking condition before it matters.
- **Week 6** — when sources disagree, find the axis they actually differ on
  rather than averaging them into a number about nothing.
- **Week 7** — a real business case, and the accept/reject/widen discipline
  for defending a number under pressure. Treat this as the rehearsal for the
  defence you give in the Court.
- **Week 8** — naming which of your inputs you share with everyone else
  answering the same brief.
- **Week 9** — who is exposed if this number is too high, who is exposed if
  it is too low, and whether that is the same party either way.

## What you produce, before the Court

A written estimate: the decomposed chain; an assumption ledger ranked by how
much each assumption moves the answer, not by how likely it feels; a stated
range with a plain-language confidence; the proxy-to-target bridge with its
breaking condition named; a note on any conflicting sources and which axis
they diverge on; a declaration of the inputs you know you share with the rest
of the cohort; and a stakes note naming who bears the cost in each direction
of error.

Alongside it, a pre-hearing disclosure: the material facts and assumptions
your estimate rests on, stated plainly enough that someone who has never seen
your working could attack them without first having to guess what you meant.
This mirrors what a real expert witness must provide — the Federal Court's
Expert Evidence Practice Note requires a report to state the material facts
and assumptions each opinion is based on, so that any challenge to it can be
aimed at something specific rather than at a guess.

**Due 7 May 2027.**

## The Court

Week 11 is not a rehearsal. It is where the estimate above is examined live,
in front of the room.

You sit in a panel with everyone who took the same brief. Your written
estimate and disclosure sit in front of you — there is no requirement to work
from memory. Every panel answers at least one question drawn from a common
pool, so every hearing is measured against something comparable, and then
faces live follow-up from the panel chair and from the other panellists.

In response to any challenge you may accept it, reject it, or widen your
range — the same three moves Week 7 trained, live instead of on paper.

**An assumption that does not survive being questioned is not, by itself, a
mark against you.** A precise, well-justified concession is frequently
stronger evidence of judgement than holding a position that should not have
held. What is assessed is the quality of your response to the challenge, not
whether your original figure emerged untouched.

## After the Court

A short revision note, applying the test Week 7 named: did something about
the world change, or only something about the room? State which challenges
moved your estimate and why, and which you held and why.

**Declining to revise, where you can justify it, is a complete answer.** This
note is not marked on how much you changed — it is marked on whether your
account of the change, or the refusal to change, actually applies the test.

**Due 21 May 2027.**

## What is marked

**Written estimate and disclosure (60%).** Whether the chain, the ledger, the
range, the bridge, the source reconciliation and the stakes note are
genuinely present and genuinely reasoned — not whether your final figure sits
close to any particular number.

**Estimate Court (20%).** The quality of your live reasoning under challenge.
A defensible concession is worth as much as a defensible hold.

**Post-Court revision (20%).** Whether your account of what changed, and why,
actually applies the world-or-the-room test.
