A rainfall total already fallen. A Census already taken. A business case
already published. Every target this course has given you so far already
existed by the time you picked up a range. Today that stops — what you commit
to next does not have an answer yet, anywhere, for anyone.

## A dated commitment

On 2 February 2021 the Reserve Bank of Australia's Governor published this,
under his own name, as the Bank's official position:

> "The Board will not increase the cash rate until actual inflation is
> sustainably within the 2 to 3 per cent target range. For this to occur,
> wages growth will have to be materially higher than it is currently. This
> will require significant gains in employment and a return to a tight labour
> market. The Board does not expect these conditions to be met until 2024 at
> the earliest."

Read it the way you would read any of this course's ranges. It names a
threshold, states the reasoning behind it, and gives a plain-language sense of
timing — not before 2024. The Bank repeated close variants of this statement
across its following meetings through the rest of 2021.

## What happened instead

On 3 May 2022 — fifteen months later — the same Governor published this:

> "At its meeting today, the Board decided to increase the cash rate target by
> 25 basis points to 35 basis points... The Board judged that now was the
> right time to begin withdrawing some of the extraordinary monetary support
> that was put in place to help the Australian economy during the pandemic."

Not before 2024 became a rise in May 2022. The Bank later acknowledged, in
public testimony, that the guidance had been heard as more certain than it
was intended to be — that people had made decisions, including on their own
mortgages, on the strength of a form of words the Bank itself would since
choose differently.

## What this is not

This is not a lesson about inflation or monetary policy, and you do not need
either to take the point. Set aside every question of why the economy moved
the way it did. What is left, once you do, is a single fact worth all your
attention: a national institution put a dated, conditional claim about the
future on the public record, and fifteen months later the record itself
supplied the score. Nobody had to argue about whether the claim held. The
calendar answered it.

## The same grammar, one variable changed

Week 4 gave you four things to write before you looked: a low bound, a high
bound, a confidence in plain words, and a line naming what would make the
range miss. Every one of those still applies today, unchanged.

What is different is where the unknown sits. In week 4 the number already
existed and only your knowledge of it was incomplete. Today the number does
not exist at all, for anyone, until the date in question arrives. Your
miss-line can no longer be about what you failed to notice — it has to name a
condition about the world between now and then.

## The class forecast

One target, shared by the whole room:

> **Canberra Airport's official daily maximum temperature on 13 May 2027**, as
> published in the Bureau of Meteorology's Daily Weather Observations for
> Canberra, ACT.

That product states plainly whose readings it carries — "Observations from
Canberra Airport" — so there is no ambiguity about which of the several
Canberra-area stations answers this. The date sits ten days after this session
and resolves well before week 12.

Before anyone checks a forecast site or a long-range outlook, write down:

- a low bound you are fairly confident the day's maximum will sit above;
- a high bound you are fairly confident it will sit below;
- your confidence, in plain words;
- one line: what would have to be true about that day for your range to miss;
- the source named above, as the thing that will resolve it.

Seal it — an envelope, a locked note, whatever your room uses. It is not
opened, checked, or spoken about again until week 12. Nobody simulates the
result between now and then, and nobody revises the bounds once they are
written down; a forecast edited after the fact is not a forecast.

This is the one Week 10 entry the Calibration Diary requires of you.

## Leaving

You have made a claim about something that has not happened yet, in the same
form the Reserve Bank used for a claim of its own. What the Bank's case adds
is the only thing forecasting ever adds to estimation: nothing to check
today, and everything to check on the date you named.
