Your written estimate and your pre-hearing disclosure are already in. Nothing
about either is rewritten today. What happens today is that both get read out
loud, in front of people who did not write them, who are allowed to ask
anything they like about what is in them.

## A duty that is not owed to whoever is paying

The Federal Court requires anyone giving expert opinion evidence to read and
be bound by a code with this clause at its centre:

> "An expert witness is not an advocate for a party and has a paramount duty,
> overriding any duty to the party to the proceedings or other person
> retaining the expert witness, to assist the Court impartially on matters
> relevant to the area of expertise of the witness."

You are not an expert witness and this is not a trial. But the panel you sit
in today borrows that duty's shape: your job is not to defend a number
because it is yours, and it is not to win. It is to show the room, honestly,
what your estimate can and cannot support.

## Why the disclosure had to come first

A panellist can only challenge an assumption they can see. That is the entire
reason your written disclosure was due before today rather than produced on
demand in the room — a challenge aimed at something you never stated is not a
challenge, it is a guess about what you might have assumed. Because the
material facts and assumptions behind your estimate are already on the
record, every question asked of you today can be pointed at something
specific you actually wrote, not at what a questioner imagines you might have
meant.

## The panel, not the witness stand

You sit with everyone who took the same brief — the same target, the same
published figures, different chains. Your written estimate and disclosure sit
in front of you; nothing here is done from memory. Every panel is asked at
least one question drawn from a pool shared across the room, so every hearing
answers something comparable before it answers anything unique to your chain.
After that, the panel chair and your fellow panellists follow up on whatever
in your disclosure they actually want to press.

To each challenge you have exactly three moves, and you already own all
three from week 7: accept it and say what new information justified the
change; reject it and say why the challenge was about you rather than about
the world; or widen your stated range without moving its centre. What is new
today is not the vocabulary. It is that the challenge is live, aimed at an
assumption someone else chose, and you cannot revise your paper before
answering it.

## What a challenge that lands actually shows

An assumption that does not survive being questioned is not, on its own,
evidence you did something wrong at the writing stage. Plenty of reasonable
assumptions fail to survive contact with a question nobody thought to ask
until the room asked it. What is being watched is what you do in the next ten
seconds: whether you can say precisely what changed your confidence, or
whether you simply fold because someone with a panel badge pushed.

Conceding cleanly, with a stated reason, is not a smaller achievement than
holding your ground. Neither is a free pass on its own — "I changed it
because they pushed" is not an answer, on the accept side any more than the
committed-anyway version was on Week 7's negotiation floor. Whichever move
you make, name what made it the right one.

## What the room leaves you with

Nobody is scored as a panel. Whatever happens to the person next to you, your
own answers are what get marked, and your own answers are what you carry out
of the room — a specific list of which of your assumptions held, which gave
way, and what exactly moved you on the ones that did. That list is the raw
material for the revision note due after the Court closes. Nothing here goes
into the Calibration Diary; there is no range committed today, only a written
one being tested.
