Eleven weeks have asked how good one estimate was, one at a time. Today asks
a different question of the same material: read as a whole set, what does it
say about the particular kind of wrong you tend to be, if there is one at
all?

## Open the one that has been sealed

Start with the entry you cannot revise. Take out your week 10 forecast — the
sealed low bound, high bound, confidence, and miss-line for Canberra Airport's
daily maximum on 20 May 2027 — and check it, for the first time, against the
Bureau of Meteorology's Daily Weather Observations for Canberra, ACT, the
exact product you named as your resolution source two weeks ago. Record
what it shows and whether your range held it, honestly, before you touch
anything else in the diary. This is the one entry in your record you were
structurally prevented from adjusting in hindsight, which is exactly what
makes it worth resolving first, while that discipline is freshest in mind.

## Count what you have

Retrieve every other entry — the one from week 4, the two copied out of
week 8's slips, and the eleven you built on your own initiative — and mark
each resolved one hit or miss. No formula does this for you. You are doing
by hand exactly what the Bureau does to its own forecasts when it checks
whether a stated 75% happened about 75% of the time: a plain count, entry by
entry, against the confidence you actually wrote down at the time.

Lay the count out where you can see the whole thing at once — a table, a
grid, whatever makes the misses visible rather than buried in prose. You are
not computing anything yet. You are making the shape of your own record
legible before you try to say what it means.

## Say what the shape is

From the count, write your pattern claim: about 400 words, naming what your
ranges have in common as a set, citing at least four specific dated entries
as evidence. A pattern is not "I was often right" — being right does not
need explaining. A pattern is something like always missing on the same side,
or being most overconfident about quantities you'd read somewhere rather than
built yourself, or widening every range that involved another person's
number. Name the actual shape, not a summary of how you felt about doing this
all semester.

## Go looking for the entry that ruins it

Before anyone else reads your claim, you read your own diary again, this
time hunting for whatever does not fit it. This is not optional and it is not
the same activity as writing the claim — confirming a pattern and searching
for its exceptions use the record in opposite directions, and doing only the
first one produces a claim that only ever meets evidence that agrees with it.
Write the counter-case honestly, about 250 words: which entries complicate
the story, and whether the pattern survives them or has to be narrowed to
survive.

## Trade diaries

Swap with a partner. Read their pattern claim and their entries independently
of what they told you their pattern was, then write one note back: either a
pattern in their record that their own claim missed, or one specific entry
that genuinely does not fit what they argued. Agreement is not a valid note —
if their claim looks airtight to you, that means you have not looked hard
enough at their exceptions yet, not that there aren't any.

## One change, stated so it can be checked

Close with about 150 words: one specific thing you will do differently the
next time you write a range, and what you would expect to see, later, if it
actually worked. Not "be more careful" — a change specific enough that a
version of you six months from now could look at your next diary and say
plainly whether you kept it.

## What today is not testing

Nobody's diary is judged on how often it was right. A diary with no misses in
it is not a strong diary; it is one whose confidences were probably never
honest in the first place. What is being read is whether your record was
genuinely kept — precommitted, dated, not touched up afterward — and whether
your account of it is honest about the entries that complicate your story as
much as the ones that support it. That is the same discipline this course
opened with, turned, for the first time, on the person who has been doing the
estimating all along.
