Two agencies, both careful, both official, both counting homelessness in
Australia. Bring nothing but attention.

## The two records

Two publications, two figures, two periods. The periods are half the session, so
they travel with the numbers everywhere below.

**Australian Bureau of Statistics** — *Estimating Homelessness: Census, 2021*.
The ABS estimates **122,494 people experiencing homelessness on Census night in
2021**, a single night in August of that year.

Nobody was asked whether they were homeless. The ABS applies rules to what
people reported about their housing that night and sorts them into six
operational groups. One group is people in severely crowded dwellings, and the
rule defining it is exact:

> "Usual residents in dwellings needing 4 or more extra bedrooms under the
> Canadian National Occupancy Standard (CNOS)."

A count of people, produced from a count of bedrooms.

**Australian Institute of Health and Welfare** — *Specialist Homelessness
Services annual report*. The AIHW records almost 289,000 clients assisted by
specialist homelessness agencies across the **financial year 2024–25**, up from
236,000 in 2011–12. Among the subset of clients whose housing status was known
at the beginning of their first support period — not the full 289,000 — the
report splits them:

> "More clients were at risk of homelessness (51% or 139,000), than experiencing
> homelessness (49% or 133,000)."

So the number to carry forward from the AIHW is **133,000** — the clients it
counted as already experiencing homelessness, rather than the full client count.

The AIHW then prints its own caution, which is exactly the one a careless reader
needs:

> "It is important to note that the number of clients supported by SHS agencies
> … reflects agency engagement and does not necessarily represent the underlying
> prevalence of homelessness …"

Before anything else, say the two periods out loud. **One night in August 2021.
One financial year running July 2024 to June 2025.** Three and a half years
apart, and one is an instant while the other is twelve months. Nothing that
follows makes sense if that slips.

## The axes

Work in pairs, publications open, and sort the difference between these two
records onto the axes below. For each one, quote or point to the thing in the
material that supports you.

- **Definition.** What has each agency declared it is counting? The ABS has six
  operational groups and a bedroom-shortfall rule. The AIHW has people who
  became clients of a funded agency, split between homeless and at risk.
- **Coverage.** Who could possibly appear in each figure? The Census reaches
  people who did not ask anyone for help. The AIHW collection reaches people who
  approached a service that reports to it, and no one else.
- **Method.** One number is derived from responses about housing circumstances.
  The other is assembled from records of support provided. Neither agency asked
  anybody the question "are you homeless".
- **Timing.** A night against a year. A count of who was in a state at an
  instant, against a count of who passed through a service across twelve
  months. These are different kinds of quantity before you even get to the
  subject matter.
- **Unit.** Persons in one case, clients in the other, and the client count is
  not a count of visits. A client may have many support periods across the
  year and across different agencies, and the collection is designed to join
  them into one client rather than count each separately — *"Matching of data
  from individual clients who presented at different agencies and/or at
  different times requires a valid statistical linkage key (SLK)"*. Designed
  to, and largely does: 99% of support periods carried a valid SLK in 2024–25,
  which is how you know this is linkage that works well rather than a
  guarantee about every record. The report adds its own caveat when it breaks
  the count down, *"noting that clients may have accessed services in more
  than one state or territory throughout the year"*, which is why the
  jurisdictions do not sum to the national figure. The unit is a client
  observed
  through service engagement across a year, not a person occupying a defined
  housing situation on one night.

Some of these you can settle from the documents. Others you will have to reason
to. Keep the two apart on the page: **what the publication states** in one
column, **what you concluded** in the other. Reasoning is welcome. Reasoning
dressed as sourcing is not.

## The trap

Look at 122,494 and 133,000.

They are close. Around eight per cent apart, which for two independent official
estimates of a hard-to-measure population would ordinarily be reassuring. Two
agencies, two methods, roughly the same answer.

Do not accept it, and spend the time working out why not.

The figures are close in the way that two thermometers in different cities in
different seasons might read the same. One is everybody the Census placed in six
housing situations on a single night in 2021. The other is people who walked
into a funded service at some point across twelve months of 2024–25 and were
homeless when they first arrived. They overlap, certainly. Neither contains the
other. And the years are different, so even the overlap is between two different
populations at two different times.

A number that agrees with yours for reasons you have not examined is more
dangerous than one that disagrees, because disagreement at least prompts a
question. This pair will pass unchallenged in any room where somebody wants them
to agree.

The move to refuse outright: averaging. There is no quantity of which 122,494
and 133,000 are two measurements, so their mean is a number about nothing.

## Pick one, for something

Three people need a figure by the end of the day. In your pair, decide which
source each should use, and — the part you have to justify, and the part we
will press hardest on — write the sentence each of them must not say.

1. A community organisation writing a funding submission for more crisis
   accommodation beds in one city.
2. A journalist writing that homelessness in Australia is rising.
3. A service manager rostering staff for the coming winter.

The answers differ, and for at least one of the three the honest response is
that neither source supports the claim being made and a third is needed. Say so
where it is true.
