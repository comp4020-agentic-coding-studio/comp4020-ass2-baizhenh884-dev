You will not estimate anything today. Bring last week's chain anyway.

The link you circled last week — the one you least wanted to defend — is
where today starts. That circle was a hunch. This week we turn it into a
question you can actually answer, by practising on an estimate nobody in this
room made.

## The case

In 1957 the Sydney Opera House was estimated at **£3.5 million**. The building
was completed in **1973**, at a cost of approximately **A$102 million**.

Resist the arithmetic. Do not divide one by the other.

Those two figures are separated by different currencies --- Australia moved to
decimal currency in 1966 --- by sixteen years of inflation, by a client decision
to change the interiors substantially, expanding the number of performance
spaces, by Jørn Utzon's departure in
1966 and a different design for the interiors that followed, and by a great deal
else besides. A ratio between them would be a number with no referent: precise,
impressive, and about nothing. If week 1 landed, that sentence should already
feel familiar.

So this is not a case study in how wrong an estimate can be. Nobody here can
apportion that gap, and this session does not try. It is a case study in
something narrower and more useful: **what was the 1957 figure resting on?**

## VERIFIED --- what the sources say

From the Sydney Opera House's own published history of the start of
construction:

- Around 700 steel-cased concrete shafts were bored into the site, with mass
  concrete filling unstable rock. Of this the official account says: *"This
  preparatory work had not been budgeted for, setting the building on a course
  of extended overruns beyond its estimated cost of 3.5 million pounds."*
- Bennelong Point's geology *"hadn't been properly surveyed when competition
  guidelines were written."* The ground turned out to be *"made of loose
  alluvial deposits permeated with seawater and completely unsuitable for
  bearing the weight of the intended structure."*
- On the roof, at that stage, *"the load they would have to bear was unknown."*
- And on the decision to begin: Premier Cahill pushed ahead *"even before
  critical design elements had been settled."*

That is the evidence. Everything else in this session is yours to argue.

## Your job: the ledger

In pairs, build an **assumption ledger** for the 1957 estimate. What must that
figure have taken for granted in order to exist at all? Some will be obvious
once written down. Some you will only find by asking what a costing needs to
know before it can produce a number.

Then rank them --- and rank them by the right thing.

**Rank by how far the answer moves if this assumption is wrong, not by how
likely you think it is to be wrong.**

Those are different orderings and the difference is the point of the week. An
assumption can be very nearly certain and still be the one carrying the whole
answer, so that the small chance it is wrong is the only risk that matters. An
assumption can be a coin flip and barely shift the result, in which case
arguing about it is entertainment. **Most consequential if wrong is not most
likely to be wrong.** People audit the second and get surprised by the first.

For each entry, keep two columns apart:

- **VERIFIED** --- what you can point to in the material above.
- **INFERRED** --- what you are reasoning to. Reasonable inference is welcome;
  passing it off as sourced is not.

Only when your ranking is on paper, compare it with what the official account
says actually went wrong early. Where did your ranking agree? Where were you
looking at the wrong assumption entirely?

## The harder question

Sort your ledger one more time, into two piles:

**Knowable in 1957.** Whether the site had been surveyed is a fact about 1957,
available to anyone who asked. An estimate resting on an unsurveyed site was
resting on something that could have been checked.

**Not knowable in 1957.** Whether a roof of that shape could be built at all,
and what it would weigh, was not a fact anyone was withholding. It was open
engineering.

The distinction matters because hindsight flattens it. From here, every
load-bearing assumption that failed looks like negligence, and that reading is
both unfair and useless --- unfair to people who could not have known, useless
because it teaches you nothing about your own estimate, whose load-bearing
assumptions are invisible to you right now for exactly the same reasons.

## Leaving

One line each, out loud: name an assumption in **your own** week 2 chain that
you now think was load-bearing, and say whether you could have known it at the
