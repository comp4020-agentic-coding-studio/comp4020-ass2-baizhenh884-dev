Nothing is invented today. The project is real, the numbers are the proponent's
own, and the criticisms were written by the agency whose job it is to make them.

## The case

The Great Western Highway runs across the Great Dividing Range through the Blue
Mountains. The 34 km between Katoomba and Lithgow is the last unimproved
stretch. In 2022 the NSW Government put a business case for upgrading the
eastern and western sections, and Infrastructure Australia evaluated it, as it
must for proposals seeking $250 million or more of Commonwealth funding.

From that evaluation, dated 8 September 2022:

> "The project is expected to generate a cost to society, with a NPV of -$579.5
> million."

The proponent's benefit–cost ratio was **0.57**. Including wider economic
benefits and land use benefits, **0.68**. A ratio below one means the costs
exceed the benefits as counted.

The project was funded anyway: **$2,030 million** from the Australian Government
in April 2022 and **$2,500 million** from the NSW Government in March 2022 —
both commitments made *before* the evaluation was published.

Sit with that for a minute before going on. Nobody involved was hiding
anything. The analysis said the project did not pay for itself, the analysis was
published, and the money had already been committed. Whatever pressure operated
here, it did not operate by concealment.

## Where the pressure actually went

Infrastructure Australia's evaluation is specific about where the appraisal
leans. Four findings, quoted:

On the largest single benefit:

> "the largest economic benefit (28% of total benefits) is derived from avoided
> routine maintenance of the existing roads and the basis of this contains some
> assumptions and methodological issues which could overstate the quantum of
> benefits in the Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA)."

On the contingency allowances applied to the two sides of the comparison:

> "The business case also assumes higher contingencies in the base case O&M (of
> 20% - P50 and 30% - P90) compared to the project case O&M (East & West
> sections: 10% - P50, 15% - P90)."

> "Overall, we consider the base case estimates of O&M costs to be
> disproportionate relative to the O&M cost estimates in the project case. This
> will have the effect of overestimating the benefits and the BCR of the
> project."

And on what happened when the first method gave the wrong answer:

> "The cost-benefit analysis (CBA) of the four shortlisted options of the whole
> program identified that the 'Do Minimum' option had the highest NPV and BCR. A
> multi-criteria analysis (MCA) was undertaken after the CBA and this
> significantly changed the prioritisation of options from that indicated by the
> CBA. The weightings and scores within the MCA implied extremely high
> valuations on some identified benefits, far higher than calculated for those
> items by the CBA. This suggests an optimism bias in the design of the MCA
> results that favours the project option."

Study the contingency finding hardest. The doing-nothing case was allowed a
generous margin for costs that might blow out; the build case was allowed a
tighter one. Each figure is arguable on its own. Set side by side they push in
one direction, and the direction is the answer somebody wanted.

That is what motivated reasoning looks like in a real document. Not a
fabrication — an asymmetry.

## Run it

Groups of four. Three of you hold the appraisal; one takes the proponent's
interest, and that person's brief is simple: **the ratio needs to clear 1.0.**

Rules, because the exercise is worthless without them. The proponent may only
ask for particular assumptions to be revisited, and must give a reason each
time. They may never ask for the answer to be changed. The pressures available
are the ones this real appraisal actually shows — the maintenance-savings
benefit, the contingency margins on either side, the 42% apportionment of
program benefits to these two sections while the Central section stays unfunded,
the wider economic benefits, and the choice of appraisal method itself.

Everybody else answers each request with exactly one of three moves, and the
group writes it down:

- **Accept** — and record what new information about the world justified it.
- **Reject** — and record why the reason offered was about the proponent rather
  than about the world.
- **Widen** — concede that you are less sure than your number implied, without
  moving the number itself.

Keep the log as you go. Do not tidy it afterwards.

## The audit

Stop the negotiation with ten minutes left and read your own log back.

For every **accept**, find the new information. If the honest entry in that
column is "they pushed twice", mark it. Nobody is graded on how few of those
they have; the skill being trained is seeing them at all, and they are far
easier to see in a log than in the moment.

Then compare what your group did with what the real business case did. Between
0.57 and 0.68 sits a decision to count additional categories of benefit — a
legitimate analytical choice, made by people who knew which direction it would
move the answer, and it still did not get them to one.

Finally, look at what Infrastructure Australia recommends to the whole sector.
Its appraisal guidance says optimism bias "refers to the underestimation of the
likelihood of an adverse event", that research "indicates that optimism bias is
a significant problem in infrastructure", and lists what to do about it,
including: ensure that underlying assumptions are transparent; compare estimates
with past comparable projects; conduct independent peer reviews.

Every one of those is a defence that works only if it is in place before the
pressure arrives. Check which ones your group had.

## Leaving
