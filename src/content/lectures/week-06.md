Somebody hands you two figures for the same thing. One says four hundred, the
other says six hundred. What do you do in the next thirty seconds?

Most people, in most rooms, reach for five hundred.

## What the average destroys

Averaging two numbers is a reasonable response to one situation: two attempts to
measure the same quantity, each carrying error that could have fallen either
way. Then the middle is a sensible guess and the spread tells you something
about your precision.

That situation is less common than the reflex suggests. Far more often the two
figures are answers to two different questions, each answered correctly. The
midpoint between them is then an answer to no question at all — a number with no
referent, arrived at by arithmetic that was never entitled to run.

What makes this expensive is not the wrong figure. It is that the gap between
the two sources was carrying information, and averaging discards it. The
distance between two careful counts tells you something real about definitions,
about who gets missed, about how the world was sampled. Collapse it to a
midpoint and you have converted evidence into a number, which is the wrong
direction of travel.

## A short list, and it is genuinely short

The reassuring thing about reconciling sources is that real disagreements
between careful publishers sit on a small number of axes, and you can learn the
list.

**Definition** — what each has declared it is counting. **Coverage** — who could
appear in the figure at all, and who is structurally invisible to it. **Method**
— survey, census, administrative record, model, derivation from something else.
**Timing** — both the date and, more slippery, the grain: a moment against a
period. **Unit** — persons, cases, events, episodes, clients, records.

Run down five axes and most disagreements resolve into "these were never the
same quantity". That answer is the reconciliation. Nothing further is owed, and
the pair of figures can go on standing side by side.

Timing is the one that catches trained people, because the date is easy to check
and the grain is easy to miss. A count of who is in a condition on one night and
a count of everyone who passed through that condition over a year are different
kinds of number. They can be close, far apart, or in either order, and none of
those outcomes tells you anything until you have noticed which is which.

## Nobody warns you at the right moment

Agencies document themselves reasonably well. The AIHW states plainly that its
client numbers reflect agency engagement rather than the prevalence of
homelessness in the community, which is exactly the caution a careless reader
needs.

But the warning lives in the publication, and the mistake happens somewhere
else — in a briefing note, a slide, a news story, a submission, where the two
figures have been lifted out and set beside each other by someone who read
neither methodology. There is no point in the pipeline where an alarm sounds.
The comparison is only ever prevented by a person who chose to go back and check
what each number was.

And when two sources happen to land close together, nothing prompts anybody to
check at all. Near-agreement reads as corroboration. Occasionally it is; more
often it is coincidence between quantities that were never comparable, and it is
the version of this error that survives longest, because it never produces an
argument.

## Choosing, rather than resolving

The output of this work is usually a decision rather than a single reconciled
figure: which source answers the question actually in front of you, and what
that source will not support.

That second half is the professional part. A source chosen well still has a
boundary, and saying where the boundary falls — this figure will carry a funding
case but will not carry a claim about a trend, this one describes service demand
but not need — is the difference between using evidence and citing it.
