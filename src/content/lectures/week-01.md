Every digit you write is an assertion about what you know. Write eight of them
and you have asserted eight, whether or not you meant to.

This is why most weak estimates are not wrong. They are *overstated*. They claim
a resolution nobody established, and they do it silently, in the notation, where
no reader thinks to look for a claim. The fix is not more care in the
arithmetic. It is asking, before the arithmetic, what resolution the decision
actually needs.

## Four questions people run together

**Precision** is how finely a number is written. **Accuracy** is how close it
sits to the thing it describes. **Uncertainty** is the spread it is really known
within, shown or not. **Sufficiency** is whether it is good enough for the
decision in front of you.

Only sufficiency is yours to settle, and it does not depend on the number at
all. It depends on what the answer is for. A figure can be imprecise, uncertain
and entirely sufficient. A figure can be precise to eight digits and useless.

## The ABS is not the villain of this lecture

It would be easy, and wrong, to run this week as *official numbers deceive
you*. The ABS publishes a population clock resolved to the individual person and
states on the same page that the projections behind it are not predictions or
forecasts. That is disclosure working exactly as it should.

What the disclosure cannot do is change how a number *looks*. Eight digits read
as eight digits' worth of knowledge, and the reader supplies the overconfidence
the publisher explicitly declined to. The lesson is about the reader. It is
about you, next time you paste a figure into something.

## Two coverage uncertainties, one census

Separately from the clock --- a different measurement, of a different thing ---
the ABS assesses how well a Census covers the population it counts. After 2021,
net undercount was 0.7% nationally and 17.4% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander people.

Hold those side by side. The methodology did not change between them. What
changed is which population is being asked about, and therefore which decisions
the resulting figure can carry. A number that is comfortably sufficient for a
national question can be the wrong tool entirely for a question about a smaller
population measured with more difficulty --- and the digits look identical
either way.

## What this course will not teach you

No hypothesis tests, no distributions, no interval arithmetic. Not because that
machinery is worthless, but because the judgment this course trains sits
upstream of it: deciding what needs to be known, how well, and saying so in
language a decision-maker can act on. You will express uncertainty in words and
ranges all semester, and you will never need a formula to do it.
