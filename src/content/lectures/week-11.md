Week 7 put you across the table from one person, in private, with a pressure
log you could revise afterward if you wanted to think it over. Today's Court
is public, panel-based, unscripted, and marked the moment you speak. The
difference is not cosmetic, and today is about why it matters.

## The paper already said this could be tested

A written disclosure exists so that a challenge can be aimed at something
specific. This is the second half of the same duty the Federal Court places
on expert reports: they must state the assumptions and material facts an
opinion rests on, so that any challenge to it can be aimed at something
specific rather than at a guess. Disclosure and
challenge are one mechanism, not two — a report that hides its assumptions
cannot be usefully cross-examined, and an examination that ignores the
report is not testing the estimate, it is testing improvisation.

## What a sentence can survive that an answer cannot

A written justification only has to survive being read. It can be dense,
qualify itself twice, and lean on a phrase doing more work than the writer
noticed — a reader moving at their own pace will often let all three pass.
A live question does not move at the reader's pace. Asked to explain, in the
next breath, exactly why one number rather than another, the same assumption
either produces a specific answer or it does not. Vagueness that reads as
thoroughness on the page reads as evasion out loud, within about two
seconds, to everyone in the room at once.

This is not a claim that written work is less honest. It is a claim that
prose and live examination are different tests, aimed at different failure
modes: prose exposes whether an argument holds together end to end; live
questioning exposes whether the person who wrote it can defend any single
joint in it, isolated, on demand, without the rest of the document to lean on.

## The question the Court is actually asking

You already know accept, reject, and widen — week 7 trained the vocabulary,
and using it again today teaches you nothing new. What is genuinely being
tested this week is something the vocabulary doesn't capture: whether the
assumption survives being said out loud to someone who is allowed to keep
pushing. A number can be perfectly defensible on paper and still belong to
someone who cannot explain, live, why it is defensible — and a number that
turns out to be wrong can still belong to someone whose live reasoning about
it was excellent throughout. The Court is built to tell those two people
apart, which a written submission on its own cannot do.
