Combining estimates is the most reliable trick in this course, and it works for
exactly one reason. Understand the reason and the failure mode follows from it
without needing to be memorised separately.

## One mechanism

Ask ten people and the errors point in different directions. Some read the
situation too high, some too low, for reasons particular to each of them — what
they happened to have seen, which comparison came to mind, which part of the
problem they thought mattered. Put the estimates together and those differences
work against each other. What survives is closer to the truth than most of the
individuals were.

That is the whole of it — errors that differ partly cancel. Whatever else gets
said about the wisdom of crowds, that arithmetic is doing all the work.

So the question to ask of any set of estimates is not how many there are. It is
whether their errors differ. Ten estimates that all lean the same way are worth
about as much as one, and cost ten times as much to collect.

## What survives averaging

Split the error in any one person's estimate into two parts: the part specific
to them, and the part they share with everyone else in the room.

Combining works on the first part and does nothing whatever to the second. If
everyone started from the same reference figure, everyone read the same briefing
note, everyone learned the technique from the same person, or everyone is
reachable by the same recruitment method, then whatever is wrong with that
common input is present in every estimate at full strength and comes through the
average untouched.

No amount of additional estimates helps, because each new one arrives carrying
the same error. This is the counter-intuitive part and the part worth holding
onto: **more data does not dilute a shared mistake.** It reproduces it.

## What tight agreement tells you

There is one further property, and it is the one that does the damage.

Independent estimates spread out. Estimates sharing an input cluster. So the
signal you are most likely to read as reassuring — everyone came out about the
same — is exactly the signal that correlation produces.

Tight agreement means one of two things: the room converged on the truth, or the
room shared something. From inside, the two look identical. You cannot tell
which by staring harder at the estimates, because the evidence that would
distinguish them is not in the numbers at all; it is in how they were produced.

The 2019 Australian polls are the cleanest available demonstration. Five final
polls, unusually close to one another, all wrong in the same direction. The
inquiry that followed could find no evidence that anybody had copied anybody. Its
explanation was that the pollsters shared a difficulty — the people who agree to
answer polls are not a cross-section of voters — and that this difficulty pushed
every one of them the same way. The convergence that would ordinarily have
signalled a well-measured result was produced by the thing that made the result
wrong.

## Auditing independence

Since agreement cannot tell you, the check has to be procedural. Before treating
a set of estimates as several pieces of evidence rather than one, ask what they
have in common:

Did they start from the same figure? Use the same dataset, or two datasets
derived from a common source? Come from people trained the same way, or by each
other? Get produced after the group had already discussed the problem? Reach
their subjects through the same channel?

A yes to any of these does not invalidate anything. It tells you how much
independent evidence you actually have, which is usually less than the count of
estimates suggests, and occasionally a great deal less.

There is no formula here and this course will not give you one. The question is
qualitative, it is answerable in a couple of minutes, and asking it at all puts
you ahead of most rooms.

## The uncomfortable version

Everything above applies to groups you are not in. It also applies to the one
you are.

Your own estimates this semester were made after the same lectures, using the
same worked examples, with the same handful of reference points in mind. Where
they agree with each other, some of that agreement is real and some of it is the
course. Distinguishing the two is not something anybody can do for you, and it
is a reasonable thing to be uncertain about.
